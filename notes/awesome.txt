Looking at awesome.csv, the related-rhyme pipeline guts (lib/rhymecrime/tuples.rb), and the contrast against the live set_related#rap output for the same cues, I think there are five patterns that turn a generic set‑related tuple into one of these. They split cleanly into "phonological" features and "semantic" features, and most of them are computable from data the build already has on disk.

Average rime length is 4.4 phonemes / 2 syllables. The vast majority of set_related#rap tuples are short single‑syllable rimes (["bling","ding","king","ring","sing"...], ["clip","dip","drip"…]) or are dominated by one productive suffix (["acclamation","accusation","allegation"…] is 39 words long, all ‑EY SH AH N). Almost no awesome entry uses a productive English suffix as its rime. The lone exception — complexion / correction / introspection / resurrection — is on ‑EH K SH AH N, but the stems (complex, correct, introspect, resurrect) are wildly different concepts. That's the cleverness premium that lets it in.

2. Onset‑only variation — minimal pairs and Spoonerisms
In a lot of these rows the entire word after the onset is identical:

handsome / ransom differ in HH/R only.
otter / rotter / water / marauder all share AA D AH R; only the onset/prefix changes.
sitz / tits is a literal Spoonerism (S/T swap).
hobby / snobby / wasabi share AA B IY and just collect different left edges.
anchored / tankard: Ø/T onset, body identical.
crypto / tiptoe: KR/T onset, body identical.
Concretely: max(len(rime) / len(pron)) across the tuple's members is ≥ 0.7 for most awesome rows. That ratio is the best one‑number summary I can find of "feels like the same word minus a consonant." Compare with a generic 39‑word ‑ation tuple, where any member's ratio is ~0.3.

3. Tuple cardinality
8 of 18 awesome rows have ≥3 words, and three have ≥4. The set‑related output for rap has hundreds of tuples but only ~10% are ≥3 once you discount the productive‑suffix monsters. Multi-word tuples are intrinsically rarer, especially once you require everything to be related to the same cue, so they earn extra credit.

4. Words live in a frequency sweet spot
Pull frequencies from word_dict.txt: every awesome member is at freq=10 (the curated common floor) except sitz and tits at freq=2 (the rare floor — those would normally be filtered into "for the desperate"). None are 999999 (function words / superhighfreq); none are below 2. The picks are vivid mid‑frequency vocabulary: tankard, marauder, rotter, skulduggery, wasabi, apostate, bituminous. Specific enough to feel chosen, common enough to land.

5. The members are tight to the cue but far from each other
This is the one that the existing classifiers aren't capturing. In each row, all members score very high on thematically_related?(cue, w) — but they scatter on different axes of the cue's semantic field:

pirate / anchored / tankard — sailing tackle + drinking. Two pirate tropes, not synonyms.
pirate / marauder / otter / rotter / water — pirate role + ocean creature + Britishism for scoundrel + the literal sea. Four facets, one rime.
transgender / apostate / prostate — heresy/identity‑rejection + body part. Metaphorical + literal.
transgender / makeup / shakeup — surface change + upheaval. Cosmetic + existential.
anime / judo / pluto — Japanese martial art + cartoon dog (or astronomical exile, take your pick). Both sit inside "stuff anime references," far from each other.
Pure cosine‑in‑Numberbatch similarity flattens this — judo and pluto aren't close in any embedding I know of. The thing that makes the row work is that both are well‑connected to anime while being far apart from each other.

Distinguishing diamonds from rough automatically
awesome.csv is currently consumed only by spec/awesome_spec.rb (a "must‑surface" gate) and listed once in notes/todo.md. It isn't yet a training signal anywhere. There's existing precedent for training a CSV‑driven classifier on top of curated labels — spec/related_spec.rb + bin/train-relatedness-classifier against curated/related.csv, and spec/rarity_spec.rb + bin/train-rarity-classifier against curated/rarity.csv. A tuple‑level classifier with awesome.csv as positives and "every other tuple in set_related#…" as negatives is the natural next step. Per‑tuple features I'd ship:

rime_phoneme_count — length of the shared rime (= length of the rime_dict key for the tuple).
rime_syllable_count — count of vowel phones in the rime.
max_post_onset_share — max_i len(rime) / len(pron(w_i)). The "spoonerism density" feature; a single number that captures whether at least one member is essentially "the rime + a consonant."
onset_diversity — distinct onset clusters across members. otter/rotter/water/marauder = 4 distinct onsets vs confess/profess/regress (mostly fricative + e + s + s) where onsets are similar.
cohort_size — total number of words in this rime in rime_dict. Productive suffixes have huge cohorts; lexical rimes have small ones. ‑EY SH AH N ≈ 500+, ‑AE N S AH M ≈ tens. Lower cohort → better diamond candidate.
cohort_density_under_cue — what fraction of the rime cohort scores thematically_related?(cue, _) == related. High density means "this rime naturally leans toward this cue," which is the structural reason pirate / buggery / skulduggery / thuggery is so satisfying — that whole rime cohort is piratical.
tuple_cardinality — k.
min_relatedness_to_cue, avg_relatedness_to_cue — scored via the existing relatedness classifier. Worst‑member score is the one that usually sinks rough tuples.
avg_pairwise_semantic_distance_among_members — Numberbatch cosine, averaged over (i, j) pairs. Counterintuitively, you want this to be not tiny — high distance between members + high relatedness to cue = the "different‑facets‑of‑the‑cue" signature.
pos_diversity, lemma_diversity — distinct POS / lemma stems across members. Penalizes morphological‑variant tuples that already largely get pruned by rhyming_tuple_redundant_with? in lib/rhymecrime/tuples.rb, but catches cases where they still leak through.
mean_log_frequency, min_log_frequency — both want to be in the mid‑band; freq=10 floors and freq=2 rarities are both fine but 999999 function‑word presence kills a tuple.
rime_is_productive_suffix — boolean, true if the rime equals a known suffix rime (‑EY SH AH N, ‑IH T IY, ‑ER, ‑IH NG, ‑D, ‑Z, etc.). One EY SH AH N exception (the complexion/correction/introspection/resurrection row) suggests this should be a soft penalty, not a hard filter.
Two cheap wins you can ship today, before training anything:

Filter or down‑rank any tuple whose rime equals a top‑20 productive English suffix in rime_dict. That single rule cuts ~70% of the bulk in set_related#rap and leaves the lexical rimes — almost all the diamonds — sitting on top.
Sort the remainder by max_post_onset_share then tuple_cardinality. Those two together capture the Spoonerism bias and the "more is better" bias that explain 16/18 of awesome.csv.
There's also a labeling consequence worth noting. The actual generated set_related#rap tuple at ["chillaxin'","jackson","klaxon","waxin'"] is a superset of the chillaxin' / jackson / waxin' row in awesome.csv — klaxon is in the cohort but didn't make the diamond. So the diamond judgement is really about which subset of a tuple feels like a coherent cluster. That's a strictly harder task than tuple‑level binary scoring, but the features above still apply: klaxon doesn't carry a rap connotation the way the ‑in' colloquials and jackson do, so its relatedness_to_cue would be lower than its tuplemates'. So a per-member "would I include this in the diamond?" classifier — features (1)-(8) plus per-member relatedness — should let you go from "tuples ≥ awesome subset" to "exactly the awesome subset."
